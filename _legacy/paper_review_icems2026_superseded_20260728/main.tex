\begin{abstract}
Paralleled virtual synchronous generators (VSGs) must support system frequency
while damping relative motion among units. When a learned controller changes
total inertia and its spatial allocation together, an apparent coordination
gain can instead come from extra aggregate support or a favorable seed. We
separate sustained common-frequency restoration, a \SI{3}{s} common-inertia
pulse, and differential inertia redistribution. The first two layers are fixed;
a parameter-shared multi-agent policy is restricted to a bounded, slew-limited,
hard-zero-sum two-area residual. A size-matched centralized actor tests whether
the multi-agent architecture adds value beyond learning itself. Both
architectures use three predefined training seeds and are fixed before an
independent 24-disturbance evaluation bank is generated.
Across 192 matched \SI{60}{s} trajectories, the centralized actor reduces
synchronization loss by \SI{24.35}{\percent} and early inter-area error by
\SI{17.04}{\percent}; the shared policy reduces them by
\SI{16.79}{\percent} and \SI{9.54}{\percent}. The 95\% intervals account for
both seed and disturbance variability and exclude zero for all four effects.
However, the shared policy is worse than the centralized actor by
\SI{9.98}{\percent} and \SI{9.04}{\percent}, respectively, with all three seeds
agreeing. All action and storage constraints are satisfied. Thus, constrained
learned differential allocation transfers to the independent evaluation bank,
but the multi-agent architecture adds no incremental value on this benchmark.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Virtual synchronous generators (VSGs) reproduce swing-like behavior in
converter controls and provide configurable virtual inertia and damping
~\cite{zhong2011synchronverters,darco2013vsm}. When several VSGs operate in
parallel, however, their average frequency and their relative motion are
different physical coordinates. Raising all inertias can improve the common
transient, whereas changing their spatial allocation can alter inter-unit and
inter-area motion. A controller that treats these effects as one score may
improve system frequency without showing improved coordination among the VSGs.

Classical work addresses complementary parts of this problem. Consensus-based
secondary control restores frequency in parallel VSG systems
~\cite{liu2020consensus,shi2021restoration}, while mutual-damping designs target
power oscillations and relative modes~\cite{fu2022mutual,gao2024mutual}. These
methods make the need for both restoration and damping clear, but they do not
by themselves answer a narrower allocation question: after the required
common support is fixed, is a disturbance-dependent differential inertia
adjustment still useful? This distinction matters because changing total
support is usually an easier route to a lower frequency-error metric than
learning where that support should be placed.

Recent learning-based VSG controllers adapt inertia and damping using
single-agent soft actor--critic~\cite{lu2024sac}, multi-agent deep deterministic
policy gradient~\cite{zhang2024maddpg}, or other multi-agent policies
~\cite{yang2023ddic,kang2025madrl}. Recent studies also coordinate virtual
inertia with load-frequency control through MARL~\cite{ali2026fuzzy} and combine
learned inertia--damping adaptation with explicit power-decoupling control
~\cite{peng2026cooperative}. These studies support model-free adaptation
under changing operating conditions. Nevertheless, jointly learning several
inertia--damping actions can mix aggregate support, differential
redistribution, and action effort. Selecting the best checkpoint can also give
an optimistic estimate if training-seed variability and a weak reference are
not separated from the claimed physical mechanism.

The submitted digest reported a DDIC reproduction and an inference-time
weighted actor ensemble. A subsequent controlled reconstruction did not isolate
an ensemble effect beyond selection of a favorable actor. The full paper
therefore omits that component and evaluates the differential coordinate
against matched causal and centralized baselines.

Evaluating power-system reinforcement learning (RL) therefore requires both
structural constraints and explicit treatment of uncertainty. Safety filters
and Lyapunov-based methods illustrate how learned controls can be restricted by
physical requirements~\cite{tabas2022safe,shuai2025safe}; statistical studies
of RL also show that point estimates alone can reverse conclusions in few-run
settings~\cite{agarwal2021statistical}.

This paper asks two questions: after sustained restoration
and aggregate transient support are fixed, does learned differential allocation
transfer to unseen disturbances, and does a multi-agent factorization add value
beyond a size-matched centralized actor? The controller is divided by both
timescale and coordinate. A slow droop--PI storage layer restores common
frequency, a short common-inertia pulse supplies aggregate fast support, and
learning can change only one two-area residual. A deterministic projection
enforces equal actions within each area and exact normalized zero-sum action
across the four VSGs.

The study makes three contributions. First, a common/differential and fast/slow
decomposition isolates the value of differential learning. Second, a
hard-zero-sum shared policy is compared with a size-matched centralized actor,
separating the effect of learning from that of multi-agent factorization.
Third, both architectures are fixed before evaluation on 24 new disturbances
with three predefined training seeds and full \SI{60}{s} trajectories; the
reported intervals account for both seed and disturbance variability.
Accordingly, the contribution is not a claim that multi-agent reinforcement
learning (MARL) is superior; it is a controlled test that separates gains from
learned differential allocation from gains attributable specifically to the
multi-agent architecture.

\section{Decoupled Control Problem}

\subsection{Common and differential coordinates}

At the electromechanical timescale, VSG \(i\) is represented by
\begin{equation}
 M_i\Delta\dot\omega_i+D_i\Delta\omega_i
 =\Delta p_{m,i}-\Delta p_{e,i},
\label{eq:swing}
\end{equation}
where \(\Delta\omega_i=(f_i-f_0)/f_0\), and the two powers are on the
\SI{100}{MVA} system base. In ANDES, \(M_i=2H_i\) is the GENCLS machine
start-up time in seconds and \(D_i\) is its per-unit damping coefficient; each
VSG proxy has \(S_n=\SI{200}{MVA}\). The normalized actions are converted to
these coefficients and written directly to \texttt{GENCLS.M/D} every
\SI{0.2}{s}, with interpolation over the internal simulation steps. These are
phasor-domain proxy parameters, not hardware ratings. The nonlinear model,
rather than~\eqref{eq:swing}, generates every reported trajectory.

For \(\Delta\mathbf f=[\Delta f_1,\ldots,\Delta f_4]^\mathsf T\), define
\begin{align}
 \Delta f_{\mathrm c}
 &=\tfrac14\mathbf1^\mathsf T\Delta\mathbf f, \label{eq:common}\\
 \Delta f_{\mathrm{AB}}
 &=\tfrac12(\Delta f_1+\Delta f_2)
  -\tfrac12(\Delta f_3+\Delta f_4), \label{eq:area}\\
 L_{\mathrm{sync}}
 &=\tfrac14\sum_{i=1}^{4}
   (\Delta f_i-\Delta f_{\mathrm c})^2 . \label{eq:sync}
\end{align}
Here \(\Delta f_i=f_0\Delta\omega_i\) is reported in hertz.
Equations~\eqref{eq:common}--\eqref{eq:sync} separate common and differential
motion. The common coordinate measures restoration and aggregate excursions;
\(\Delta f_{\mathrm{AB}}\) and \(L_{\mathrm{sync}}\) measure differential
motion. The control design prevents a learned reduction in
\eqref{eq:sync} simply by increasing every \(M_i\).

\subsection{Fixed common layers}

The slow layer requests equal active power from four storage devices:
\begin{equation}
 P^\star_{i,k}=\Pi_{\mathcal F}\!\left[
 K_p(f_0-f_{\mathrm c,k})+
 K_i\sum_{\tau=0}^{k}(f_0-f_{\mathrm c,\tau})\Delta t
 \right],
\label{eq:drooppi}
\end{equation}
In~\eqref{eq:drooppi}, \(K_p=2.0\) system-pu/Hz/device,
\(K_i=0.2\) system-pu/(Hz\,s)/device, and \(\Pi_{\mathcal F}\) enforces
power, ramp, converter-capability, state of charge (SOC), and energy limits
with conditional
anti-windup. These gains were selected in the preceding development stage and
held fixed for the 24-case evaluation; they are not claimed as universal
optima. This layer is responsible for sustained restoration.

The fast common layer is a fixed normalized pulse \(c_k=0.25\) for the first
15 samples (\SI{3}{s}) and zero afterwards. With \(M_0=200\),
\(\Delta M_{\max}=600\), and fixed \(D_i=100\), the common-only reference is
\(M_i=M_0+\Delta M_{\max}c_k\). The slow layer is evaluated against zero
support, and the pulse is then evaluated against the slow-only reference
before learning is introduced. A development-stage outcome-seeing screen
preferred the selected pulse in five of eight cases; the pulse was then fixed
before the 24-case comparison.

\section{Hard-Zero-Sum Learning Architectures}

\subsection{Executed action}

One memoryless actor \(\mu_\theta\) is shared by four agents. Each invocation
maps a seven-dimensional local observation to
\(z_{i,k}\in[-1,1]\). The execution layer aggregates the four votes as
\begin{align}
 q^{\mathrm{raw}}_k
 &=\frac{q_{\max}}{2}
 \left[\operatorname{mean}(z_{1,k},z_{2,k})
 -\operatorname{mean}(z_{3,k},z_{4,k})\right],\label{eq:qraw}\\
 q_k&=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(q^{\mathrm{raw}}_k,
 q_{k-1}-\Delta q_{\max},q_{k-1}+\Delta q_{\max}\right),
 \label{eq:qclip}\\
 \mathbf r_k&=q_k[1,1,-1,-1]^\mathsf T ,
\label{eq:projection}
\end{align}
A final clip enforces \(|q_k|\le q_{\max}\), where
\(q_{\max}=\Delta q_{\max}=0.25\). During the active window,
\begin{equation}
 M_{i,k}=M_0+\Delta M_{\max}(c_k+r_{i,k}),\qquad D_{i,k}=100 .
\label{eq:physical_action}
\end{equation}
After \SI{3}{s}, \(c_k=\mathbf r_k=\mathbf0\). Because the magnitude and slew
constraints act on the scalar \(q_k\), within-area equality and
\(\mathbf1^\mathsf T\mathbf r_k=0\) cannot be broken by elementwise clipping.
Equations~\eqref{eq:qraw}--\eqref{eq:projection} define the scalar projection;
the physical mapping in~\eqref{eq:physical_action} is checked separately for
finite-precision error.

Each agent observes local frequency deviation, common frequency deviation,
local-minus-common deviation, local rate of change of frequency (RoCoF), its
previous signed residual, local electrical-power change since reset, and a
fixed area sign. Each component is scaled in physical units and clipped to
\([-5,5]\). Thus the actors share
parameters but receive different local measurements; execution still requires
the small coordination layer in~\eqref{eq:projection} to aggregate their
votes.

\subsection{Matched training objective}

The shared controller uses one 64--64 actor (\(4737\) parameters) at all four
agents and one pair of centralized twin-delayed deep deterministic
policy gradient (TD3) critics~\cite{fujimoto2018td3}. A
matched centralized controller maps the concatenated 28-dimensional
observation directly to \(q_k\) through a 55--55 actor (\(4731\) parameters).
Both controllers use the same critic architecture and hyperparameters,
execution map, training data, optimizer settings, replay schedule, exploration
process, and team reward. The critics receive the joint observation and
executed scalar \(q_k/q_{\max}\), following the centralized-critic principle
~\cite{lowe2017maddpg}. The reward is
\begin{equation}
 R_k=-\frac{L_{\mathrm{sync},k}}{2(0.05)^2}
 -\frac{\Delta f_{\mathrm{AB},k}^2}{2(0.05)^2}
 -0.01\left(\frac{q_k-q_{k-1}}{0.25}\right)^2 .
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
No terminal-frequency, RoCoF, or storage term is included in
\eqref{eq:reward}; those quantities are assessed independently.

Critics use 64--64 hidden units; both learning rates are
\(3\times10^{-4}\). The discount, target-update factor, replay capacity,
batch size, and warm-up are \(0.99\), \(0.005\), \(10^5\), 256, and 512
transitions. Policy noise, clipping noise, exploration noise, and the delayed
update period are \(0.1\), \(0.2\), \(0.1\), and 2. Seeds 17, 53, and 89 are
each trained for 300 episodes of 15 steps. Only the six final checkpoints are
evaluated; there is no best-checkpoint selection or early stopping.

\section{Experimental Setup and Evaluation}

\subsection{Plant and disturbance bank}

All experiments use ANDES 2.0.0~\cite{cui2021andes} and a modified two-area
Kundur system~\cite{kundur1994} with four VSG proxies. Each PV+GENCLS proxy is
paired with an independent grid-following ESD1 storage device at buses 12, 16,
14, and 15; this arrangement is not a unified physical grid-forming battery
energy storage system. Each storage device is rated
\SI{36}{MVA}/\SI{28}{MWh}, has a 0.36-system-pu power limit, a one-second
external ramp, and SOC limits of 0.2--0.8 from an initial 0.5. The rating is a
50-fold system-study scaling of the \SI{720}{kVA}/\SI{560}{kWh} experimental
unit reported in~\cite{gerini2022gfm}; the explicit SOC, converter, and ramp
limits follow storage-modeling practice summarized in
~\cite{wecc2016bess}.

The 24-case development bank covers four disturbance locations, both signs,
and moderate (0.65--0.85 pu), strong (0.95--1.15 pu), and edge
(1.35--1.50 pu) magnitudes. It is used for classical mechanism checks and
training only. After all six controllers are fixed, an independent generator
(seed 2026072704) creates the independent evaluation bank with the same balanced
location/sign/severity design but new magnitudes. A zero-support screen that
does not use controller outcomes accepts all 24 cases; no case is redrawn or
excluded. The control interval is
\SI{0.2}{s}. Training uses the \SI{3}{s} active window, whereas every
evaluation trajectory runs for \SI{60}{s} and reports the simulator's physical
\SI{60}{Hz} frequency.

\subsection{Physical checks and evaluation rule}

Before testing learned controllers, we verify each physical prerequisite. The
slow-layer check compares droop--PI storage with zero requested storage power.
The fast-layer check adds the common-inertia pulse above the slow layer. A
four-arm comparison of zero support, the slow layer, the fast layer, and both
layers tests whether their benefits are additive or synergistic. Finally, an
outcome-seeing oracle selects the best of six fixed differential pulses or the
reference for each case. Because this selection uses future information, the
oracle provides only an upper bound on attainable differential benefit.

The evaluation-bank test compares eight arms: the fixed common layers with
\(q=0\), a bounded causal area-RoCoF baseline, three centralized TD3 seeds,
and three shared-MARL seeds, giving 192 scenario-matched trajectories. The
causal controller uses only the current joint observation:
\begin{equation}
 q_k^{\mathrm c}=q_{\max}\tanh\!\left[
 -k_f\frac{\Delta f_{\mathrm{AB},k}}{0.05}
 -k_r\frac{\Delta\dot f_{\mathrm{AB},k}}{0.5}\right].
\label{eq:causal}
\end{equation}
The denominators are in hertz and hertz per second. Nine gain pairs were fixed
before the development comparison; among candidates meeting all checks, the
lowest worst-location score selected \((k_f,k_r)=(0,0.25)\). This law was then
fixed before the independent bank and passed through the same magnitude, slew,
active-window, and physical-action mapping as the learned controllers.

The two primary endpoints are the full-trajectory mean of
\eqref{eq:sync} and
\(\sum_{k=1}^{15}|\Delta f_{\mathrm{AB},k}|\Delta t\).
Secondary checks are maximum sampled RoCoF, worst-bus peak, full-horizon
common-frequency integral absolute error (IAE), and mean common error in the
final \SI{10}{s}. A controller is considered effective only if both primary
effects improve by at least 2\% and both 95\% interval upper bounds remain
below zero. Fast
metrics may not worsen by more than 5\%, and slow metrics by more than 2\%.
Completion and limits on action magnitude, slew, active-window compliance,
storage power and energy, SOC, saturation, constraints, and empirical 90\%
conditional value at risk (CVaR90) must also pass.

\section{Results and Discussion}

Figure~\ref{fig:effects} summarizes the classical mechanism checks and the
evaluation-bank architecture comparisons.

\subsection{Classical authority and attainability}

The slow layer materially restored the common coordinate. Relative to zero
support, it reduced full-horizon mean-frequency IAE by
\SI{58.63}{\percent} (95\% interval
\SIrange{-58.67}{-58.58}{\percent}) and final-window common error by
\SI{77.29}{\percent}
(\SIrange{-77.36}{-77.22}{\percent}); all 24 pairs improved on both endpoints.
Above this established slow layer, the common-inertia pulse reduced maximum
RoCoF by \SI{28.37}{\percent}, worst-bus peak by
\SI{11.08}{\percent}, synchronization loss by \SI{4.29}{\percent}, and early
inter-area IAE by \SI{9.91}{\percent}, with all paired intervals below zero.

The four-arm comparison showed additive rather than synergistic behavior. None
of six interaction terms reached the required \(-2\%\) improvement with an
interval below zero; the interaction estimates range from
\(-0.15\%\) to \(+1.49\%\) of the zero-support mean. Hence the two classical
layers are worth retaining, but their joint value is not evidence of a special
coordination synergy.

The outcome-seeing oracle then tested whether any differential action could
still help. It chose a non-reference differential pulse in 18 of 24
cases and reduced synchronization loss by \SI{25.64}{\percent} and early
inter-area IAE by \SI{19.66}{\percent}, with paired intervals wholly below
zero. These results establish an attainable differential margin on the
development bank; they do not establish that a causal policy can identify the
action.

The bounded causal area-RoCoF baseline in~\eqref{eq:causal} met the criterion
on neither endpoint. Its
synchronization-loss effect was \(\CausalSyncEffect\%\) (95\% interval
\(\CausalSyncCI\%\)); its area-IAE effect was \(\CausalAreaEffect\%\)

Centralized TD3 reduced synchronization loss by \SI{24.35}{\percent} and area
IAE by \SI{17.04}{\percent}. Shared MARL also met the criterion on both
endpoints, with
\SI{16.79}{\percent} and \SI{9.54}{\percent} reductions. These intervals
include both seed and scenario variability and exclude zero.

The direct architecture comparison showed that shared MARL was worse
than the centralized actor by \SI{9.98}{\percent} in synchronization loss and
\SI{9.04}{\percent} in area IAE. The intervals lie entirely above zero, and
each of the three paired seeds favors centralized TD3 on both endpoints. The
learned differential action is therefore directionally consistent across the
three predefined seeds, but the multi-agent factorization is not needed to
explain the improvement on this bank.

All 192 trajectories completed 300 samples. Maximum commanded storage power was
\(\FormalMaxCommand\) pu, SOC remained in
\([\FormalMinSoc,\FormalMaxSoc]\), and no saturation reason or constraint
violation occurred. Action magnitude, slew, active-window, relative no-harm,
and all storage limits were satisfied. The hard-zero-sum map prevents either
learned controller from obtaining differential improvement by increasing
fleet-average inertia.

Figure~\ref{fig:dynamics} makes the coordinate constraint visible. Each learned
scalar \(q_k\) moves the two area inertias in opposite directions while their
fleet mean remains equal to the reference allocation. Common frequency and
aggregate storage power remain close to \(q=0\), so the illustrated
differential response is not produced by additional common support. At
\SI{3}{s}, the common pulse and differential residual are switched off by
design; the jump is not closed-loop settling. Statistical inference comes from
all 24 scenarios and all three seeds in Fig.~\ref{fig:effects}, not from this
trace.

\section{Limitations and Reproducibility}

The independent evaluation bank and three seeds address disturbance and seed
robustness within one modified Kundur topology; they do not establish topology
generalization. Three predefined seeds rule out a single lucky-seed explanation
on this bank but do not characterize the wider training-seed population.
Because the shared execution layer aggregates four local votes,
the result also does not support fully decentralized deployment. Zero-sum
projection guarantees fleet-budget neutrality at the input, not modal or
dynamic decoupling of the nonlinear plant. The phasor-domain hybrid actuator
does not provide electromagnetic-transient validation, a unified grid-forming
battery implementation, a stability certificate, or hardware validation.

The initial numerical check rejected five of 192 rows because single-precision
slew values exceeded 0.25 by at most \(\num{\AuditMaxExcess}\), less than one
unit in the last place, \(\num{\AuditOneUlp}\), at that limit. A
representation-aware correction changed only those five Boolean checks; all
192 stored trajectory hashes, checkpoints, efficacy statistics, and thresholds
were retained. The corrected check and complete experiment provenance are
generated directly from the retained artifacts.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper separates common restoration, common transient support, and
differential inertia redistribution in a four-VSG MARL study. The slow
droop--PI storage layer restored common frequency, the common-inertia pulse
improved the fast transient, and an outcome-seeing oracle confirmed an
attainable differential margin. On the independent evaluation bank, both
centralized TD3 and shared MARL improved synchronization and early inter-area
error across three predefined seeds while satisfying every evaluated physical
limit.
However, the size-matched centralized actor was consistently stronger. The
evidence therefore supports constrained learned differential allocation, not
the necessity of MARL or closed-loop modal decoupling. This prevents the
learning gain from being misattributed to an architecture or physical
mechanism.
